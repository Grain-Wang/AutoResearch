# RoSAMDepth: Robust Self-supervised Depth Estimation Leveraging Segment Anything Model

[Open source PDF](../reference_papers_origin/Gao_RoSAMDepth_Robust_Self-supervised_Depth_Estimation_Leveraging_Segment_Anything_Model_CVPR_2026_paper.pdf)

## Abstract

Robust depth estimation aims to maintain high-quality depths across diverse conditions. However, most existing methods estimate depth without taking into account the object-level information. As a result, the predicted depth may easily deviate within objects and become blurred under adverse conditions. To overcome this weakness, we propose RoSAMDepth, a novel framework that can assist robust self-supervised depth estimation in leveraging rich and diverse object-level priors from the Segment Anything Model (SAM). We focus on incorporating object-level information across three key aspects: a segment-guided representation contrasting method that injects object-level awareness into the feature representation space; an adaptive regional outlier masking strategy combined with a regional Gaussian likelihood loss that enforces regional depth smoothness; and an object-level reliability estimation strategy that mitigates the influence of unreliable supervision. Extensive experiments across multiple datasets and diverse weather conditions demonstrate that our method produces sharper, more accurate depth predictions, consistently outperforming state-of-the-art methods. Our code is available at https://github.com/xagao/RoSAMDepth.

### 1. Introduction

Monocular depth estimation is a fundamental task in computer vision, crucial for downstream applications, such as autonomous driving, robotics, and AR/VR. Supervised methods [1, 5, 15, 17, 26] achieve high accuracy but rely on expensive ground-truth (GT) depths for training. To alleviate this dependency, self-supervised methods leverage geometrical constraints from stereo pairs or monocular videos to learn depth estimation without explicit annotations [2, 7, 8, 11, 22, 36, 40, 43]. *Corresponding Author: Wei Liu Day Night Rain Image & SAM Masks Syn2Real-Depth Ours Figure 1. Our RoSAMDepth utilizes object-level information from SAM [14] to generate sharp and accurate depths across diverse conditions. Despite their success under standard daytime conditions, self-supervised methods often degrade in challenging conditions such as nighttime or adverse weather [7, 21, 28, 37], where the photometric consistency breaks. Early works address a specific condition (e.g., nighttime), using adversarial learning or feature disentanglement for domain representations [18, 30, 32]. Recent methods [7, 21, 28, 37] focus on unified robustness across diverse degradations by synthesizing paired data with teacher–student learning or further adapting synthetic-trained teachers to real degraded data. However, existing methods typically treat degraded regions uniformly, without considering the semantic or object-level differences that significantly influence visual perception and depth estimation. As shown in Fig. 1, ignoring object-level priors can lead to blurred depth boundaries and unstable predictions under complex environments or diverse conditions. To better estimate depth across diverse real data, it is important to understand the object spatial relationships and assess the reliability of depth cues. Meanwhile, the predicted depth should also be smooth only within the same continuous object region, while allowing sharp depth boundaries near object edges. Thus, incorpo-

rating object-level information is key to refining robust selfsupervised depth estimation under adverse conditions. Indeed, prior methods exploit semantic segmentation to incorporate object information for depth estimation under standard conditions [12, 19]. However, semantic segmentation is inherently limited, as it cannot distinguish instances within the same class, which may have drastically different depths. It is also constrained by a limited set of predefined classes, often failing to recognize novel objects in the open world. Moreover, segmentation models trained on specific domains or weather conditions generalize poorly across diverse scenarios. In comparison, the Segment Anything Model (SAM) [14] offers a better option. SAM is a general-purpose image segmentation model pre-trained on massive and diverse datasets. It separates individual instances and demonstrates strong zero-shot robustness to unseen objects and various weather conditions [14, 25, 29]. Building upon these advantages, we propose to integrate SAM for additional object-level information to enhance robust self-supervised depth estimation. Our framework jointly improves feature representation and depth learning. Specifically, for representation learning, we propose segment-guided representation contrasting (SRC) that exploits SAM masks for implicitly embedding object-level structural awareness into the feature space. Furthermore, to improve supervision for depth learning, we identify key limitations in the conventional smoothness loss and the pixel-level pseudo-label quality indicator. In response, we introduce two strategies: an adaptive regional outlier masking (AROM) strategy paired with a regional Gaussian likelihood loss to rectify inconsistent depth outliers; and an object-level reliability estimation (ORE) strategy that mitigates the influence of unreliable supervision signals. Our method significantly outperforms previous methods in diverse conditions through different real-world datasets [3, 20]. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to effectively integrate SAM into robust self-supervised depth estimations, using only pre-generated SAM masks during the training as object-level priors. • We propose three novel designs to take full advantage of SAM’s object-level priors: segment-guided representation contrasting (SRC), adaptive regional outlier masking (AROM) strategy combined with a regional Gaussian constraint, and object-level reliability estimation (ORE) strategy. These components provide efficient guidance to train the model and lead to improved depth. • Extensive experiments on the nuScenes [3] and Oxford Robotcar [20] benchmarks demonstrate that our method consistently outperforms state-of-the-art approaches, delivering sharper and more accurate depth under diverse real-world conditions.

### 2. Related Work

#### 2.1. Self-supervised Monocular Depth Estimation

Self-supervised monocular depth estimation methods use multi-view images for training and learn to estimate the depth from a single input image with photometric reconstruction consistency as supervision. SfM-Learner [43] firstly introduces an unsupervised paradigm that relies on photometric consistency, formulating depth estimation as a view synthesis task. Monodepth2 [8] introduces the per-pixel photometric loss with an auto-mask and a fullresolution sampling for training the model to reduce the impact of moving objects. Several methods [4, 9, 12, 19] use extra semantic information to improve performance, and the frameworks explored in [16, 38] jointly learn depth, camera pose, and optical flow. Additionally, the multi-frame monocular depth estimation is handled in [10, 36], which predicts more accurate depths by taking two frames of a monocular video as the input.

#### 2.2. Depth Estimation under Adverse Conditions

Robust depth estimation under adverse conditions, such as rain, night, and fog, remains challenging and actively studied. Early approaches focus on nighttime. DeFeat-Net [30] jointly learns depth and cross-domain features, ADIDS [18] utilizes a generative adversarial network (GAN) to generate paired nighttime images and splits features into private and invariant sub-spaces, and ADFA [32] employs a GANbased discriminator with adversarial training to align nighttime and daytime features. Later methods like RNW [35] and STEPS [41] integrate image enhancement to improve depth quality, and WSGD [33] combines denoising with a lighting change decoder to predict per-pixel changes. More recent methods extend beyond nighttime to tackle multiple conditions by exploiting generative techniques such as GANs and diffusion models [27, 39, 42, 44]. Md4all [7], Robust-Depth [28], and WeatherDepth [34] enhance robustness through GAN-based weather augmentations integrated into teacher-student frameworks. Complementary to these approaches, DM-MDE [31] incorporates stable diffusion priors, and Syn2RealDepth [37] proposes an auxiliary space and a real adaptation stage. Our key insight is that the fidelity of synthetic-to-real adaptation can be significantly improved with explicit object-level guidance. We operationalize this by incorporating cues from SAM, which enhance depth estimation in two ways: 1) enabling the model learning object-aware perception, and 2) providing objectlevel priors for supervision signals refinement.

#### 3.2. Object-Aware Representation Learning

To inject object-level awareness into the depth network, we introduce segment-guided representation contrasting (SRC). Our SRC transfers segmentation priors from SAM through a regional prototype-based contrastive approach. It aims to encourage the depth network to infer geometric coherence, ensuring that pixels within the same segment are assigned structurally consistent depths. Specifically, SRC exploits SAM from a clustering perspective, contrasting the features of the depth network according to the given segments of the SAM. A key design choice of SRC is to perform the contrasting at the feature level rather than imposing direct constraints on the final depth. This is because SAM tends to oversegment a single object into multiple parts, and enforcing depth discontinuities at every segment boundary could lead to erroneous supervision. Instead, by transferring segmentation priors into the depth decoder’s feature space, SRC allows the network to implicitly learn object-aware representations without explicitly altering the depths. Fig. 2 illustrates the proposed SRC. The depth decoder produces a pyramid of multi-scale features. To incorporate segmentation cues into each decoder stage, we first resize the SAM masks using nearest interpolation to match the resolution of each feature level, denoted as M (s) sam ∈ {0, 1}N× H 2s × W 2s . At each scale s, we generate a prototype for each segment M (s) sam,i by averaging the features of the pixels located on the segment: pt(s) i = 1 |M (s) sam,i| X (x,y)∈M(s) sam,i F(s) x,y, (1) where F(s) x,y denotes the feature at position (x, y) in scale s, and pt(s) i is the prototype of the ith segment. Note that we normalize the features/prototypes along the channel dimension before and after averaging, to ensure they are constrained to lie on the unit hypersphere. Then, we enforce each pixel feature to be close to the prototype of the segment it belongs to and distant from the prototypes of other segments. This contrastive objective encourages features within the same segment to form compact clusters, enabling the depth network to learn object-aware representations that lead to more consistent depth predictions. We formalize this contrasting process with the InfoNCELoss [23] as: Lsrc = − S X s=1 N X i=1 X (x,y)∈M(s) sam,i F(s) x,y · pt(s) i /T PN j=1 F(s) x,y · pt(s) j /T , (2) where S is the number of feature scales, N is the total number of segments in the Msam, and T is the temperature. #### 3.3. Depth Learning with Object-Level Priors

To further enhance the quality of predicted depth, we leverage object-level priors to improve depth learning signals. First, we propose to achieve object-aware smoothness by combining an adaptive regional outlier masking (AROM) strategy with a regional Gaussian likelihood loss. This combination suppresses intra-region outliers and maintains regional smoothness. Second, we develop an objectlevel reliability estimation (ORE) strategy for pseudo-label reweighting, which assesses depth reliability at the object level rather than the pixel level. These components together enforce region-consistent supervision and substantially improve the depth quality in diverse conditions. Object-aware Smoothness. This adaptation of the supervision signal comes from the motivation that object segmentation can be used to formulate a more precise smoothness constraint, thereby better regularizing the depth map. We begin by discussing the shortcomings of the conventional smoothness loss and then show how we mitigate those limitations with the help of SAM. Limitations of previous smoothness losses. Most existing methods define smoothness loss using first-order depth derivatives [7, 8, 36, 43]. Given mean-normalized inverse depth d∗= d/d, the conventional smoothness loss is: Lsm = |∂xd∗| e−|∂xI| + |∂yd∗| e−|∂yI|, (3) in which e−|∂I| serves as the edge-aware weights to avoid penalties across object boundaries, where depth is not necessarily continuous. Such weights are usually derived from the image edges, which often coincide with object boundaries [8, 43]. However, the inherent limitations of the boundary-aware smoothness loss prevent it from leveraging more accurate object boundaries. One example is illustrated in Fig. 3. As shown in Fig. 3b, the depth estimation fails around people, resulting in blurred depth and erroneously predicting part of the background building as a continuous foreground. In this case, the gradient of the boundary-aware smoothness loss mostly

arises near the depth boundary (Fig. 3c), since the loss mainly focus on where the image is smooth (e−|∂I| is large) and the depth exhibits disconunity (|∂d∗| is large). However, in regions where the depth is incorrectly estimated but locally smooth (e.g., mispredicted background between the object boundary and the erroneous depth boundary), the loss incorrectly assumes depth continuity (|∂d∗| is small) and fails to respond, resulting in weak gradients confined to a limited neighborhood around the depth boundary. Regional smoothness based on segment. To address this limitation, we extend the smoothness formulation from object boundaries to object regions. Intuitively, the inaccurate depth values in Fig. 3b can be understood as regional outliers within the same object (background building). To achieve regional smoothness, we introduce an adaptive regional outlier masking (AROM) strategy together with a regional Gaussian likelihood loss. Specifically, AROM computes an outlier-suppressed mask Mout ∈[0, 1]H×W that filters out depth outliers based on intra-object geometric consistency. To obtain Mout, for each object Msam,i, we first calculate the mean dt,i and standard deviation σt,i of the inverse teacher depth dt. Then we compute a dense deviation map δ ∈RH×W , where each pixel’s normalized deviation from its regional mean is obtained by: δ = N X i Msam,i · |dt −dt,i| σt,i . (4) A fixed threshold on δ to identify outliers is suboptimal, as a small value tends to misclassify large but structurally consistent depth variations (e.g., ground or building surfaces) as outliers, while a large one fails to detect the outliers in homogeneous regions. We therefore introduce an adaptive threshold τ ∈RH×W that adjusts dynamically to each object’s intrinsic depth variability. It relaxes the outlier criterion in high-variance regions (large σt,i) while enforcing a stricter one in homogeneous regions (small σt,i): τ = N X i Msam,i · (τ0 + λσt,i) , (5) where τ0 is a base threshold and λ controls the sensitivity. The resulting Mout assigns low values to depth outliers (Fig. 3d). With Mout, we introduce a regional Gaussian likelihood loss, which models the inverse teacher depth dt within each segment Msam,i as a Gaussian distribution characterized by dt,i and σt,i. A widely adopted strategy for evaluating the reliability of pseudo-labels is to compute the pixel-wise photometric error [21, 28], assuming that low local errors correspond to trustworthy predictions. However, this local metric is misaligned with the nature of depth reliability, which typically exhibits strong object-level spatial correlations. As illustrated in Fig. 4b and d, scaling the depth of a car by different factors results in only localized deviations of the indicator (photometric error in Eq. (8)), while the interior region remains falsely reliable. This is because pixels within the same object often share similar appearances, leading to chance alignments even when the object’s depth is mispredicted. Consequently, pixel-level evaluation identifies only scattered unreliable pixels and fails to capture object-level unreliability. Furthermore, when selecting depths corresponding to the lowest indicator values as in [21, 28], many depths on incorrectly scaled factors are still considered trustworthy, producing incorrectly selected depths (see Fig. 4c). ### 4. Experiments

#### 4.1. Implement Details

Both our teacher and student depth networks adopt the same architecture [36] as in [37]. In our implementation, we use the pretrained teacher model provided by [37], which is trained on synthetic data, without any fine-tuning. The student network Φs is initialized from the teacher Φt and trained for 10 epochs following [37]. The model is implemented in Pytorch [24] and optimized using Adam [13] with a batch size of 10. The learning rate is initialized to 8×e−5 and decayed by a factor of 0.5 every 5 epochs. For the Segment Anything Model [14], we use the off-the-shelf default ViT-H pretrained model. Additional implementation details are provided in the supplementary material.

#### 4.2. Dataset and Evaluation Metric

We evaluate our method on the nuScenes [3] and Oxford Robotcar [20] datasets and follow the same training data schedules as [37]. NuScenes [3] is a comprehensive benchmark with real driving data under daytime, nighttime, and rainy conditions. In our experiments, we utilize 21,476 images for training, including 15,129 daytime, 3,796 rainy, and 2,551 nighttime frames. The evaluation set consists of 6,019 consecutive frame pairs: 4,449 daytime, 1,088 rainy, and 602 nighttime. We report results in both single-frame and multi-frame settings, and all metrics are computed for ground truth within 0.1–80 m. For the Oxford RobotCar [20] dataset, following WSGD [33], we use 17,790 daytime and 19,162 nighttime images for training, and 1,411 single images for testing (702 daytime images and 709 nighttime images). Evaluation is performed under the single-frame setting, with metrics computed within 0.1–50 m. We employ commonly adopted metrics [7, 8, 31, 35, 37] for depth estimation, including absolute relative error (AbsRel), root mean square error (RMSE), squared relative error (SqRel), and threshold accuracy (δ1).

#### 4.3. Quantitative Results

Our quantitative testing results across different datasets are shown in Tabs. 1 and 2. Our method significantly outperforms state-of-the-art unsupervised methods on both nuScenes [3] and Oxford Robotcar [20] datasets on almost all evaluation metrics. On the nuScenes dataset [3], which covers diverse conditions including day-clear, night, and day-rain. Following [37], we evaluate both single-frame (identical inputs) and multi-frame (temporally adjacent inputs) settings. Our method consistently demonstrates superior performance across almost all metrics and conditions. Compared with recent methods trained on synthetic data [7, 31], our method significantly outperforms them. When compared to the real-data-trained method [37], our model achieves consistent improvements across almost all metrics under both single-frame and multi-frame settings, with average relative gains of 2.8%, 2.7%, 0.6%, and 0.9% on AbsRel, SqRel, RMSE, and δ1, respectively, averaged over all conditions and both single- and multi-frame settings. In Tab. 2, we present a comparative analysis on the Robotcar dataset [20], evaluating the performance of previous methods against our method. Since the Oxford Robotcar test set contains only single-frame samples, we evaluate under the single-frame setting. Our method achieves substantial improvements of 4.4%, 7.6%, 4.7%, 0.9% on AbsRel, SqRel, RMSE, and δ1, respectively, compared with [37]. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of our method, which leverages SAM priors to improve the depth quality and robustness in self-supervised depth estimation under diverse conditions.

### 6. Conclusion

This work presents RoSAMDepth, a novel robust selfsupervised monocular depth estimation framework that leverages object-level priors from SAM during training. Our method integrates three key components: segmentguided representation contrasting (SRC) for structural awareness, adaptive regional outlier masking (AROM) with regional Gaussian likelihood loss for consistent depth, and object-level reliability estimation (ORE) for reliabilityaware learning. Experiments on challenging datasets demonstrate consistent improvements over existing methods across diverse conditions.
