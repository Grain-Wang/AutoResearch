# Iris: Integrating Language into Diffusion-based Monocular Depth Estimation

## Abstract

Conventional monocular depth estimators suffer from visual ambiguities and nuisances. We demonstrate that language can improve the fidelity of estimates by providing additional information through text as a condition, thereby reducing the solution space for depth estimates. This conditional distribution is learned during text-to-image pretraining of diffusion models. To generate images under various viewpoints and layouts that reflect textual descriptions, the model implicitly encodes object shapes and their spatial relationships, comprising the 3-dimensional (3D) scene structure. We investigate the benefits of integrating text descriptions into the training and inference of the diffusion-based monocular depth estimators (MDE). We experiment with three different diffusion-based MDEs, namely Marigold, Lotus, and E2E-FT, and their variants. By training on HyperSim and Virtual KITTI, and evaluating on NYUv2, KITTI, ETH3D, ScanNet, and DIODE, we find that our strategy of integrating text into MDEs improves the overall accuracy of depth estimates, especially in small areas. The effect is also targeted in that improvements are more pronounced in specific regions described in the text. Naturally, this lends to iterative refinement of the depth estimate by providing additional descriptions of the 3D scene. Separately, we find that incorporating text can accelerate convergence of training and the inference diffusion trajectory. 1

Language improves monocular depth estimation for the described small (and visually ambiguous) regions. Separately, due to image processing, whether through a diffusion model or conventional filtering, objects that occupy small areas tend to be smoothed out or entirely missed, leading to a loss of fidelity in fine details such as thin structures and distant objects. Hence, it is difficult for conventional monocular depth estimators to discern shapes, sizes, and spatial relationships amongst objects populating a 3D scene, without additional assumptions of regularities. Given that one is aware of the collection of objects populating the 3D scene, can depth estimates of a monocular depth estimator be regularized to address the aforementioned limitations? We hypothesize that knowing the presence of an object within a 3D scene plays a role in localizing, and in this case, more faithfully reconstructing it, despite the object being visually less pronounced. Intuitively, with knowledge of typical shapes of objects present, one may also produce more regular estimates in regions where there exists visual ambiguities (e.g., repetitive or homogeneous regions). Furthermore, the existence of objects not included in such a collection may be inferred based on cooccurrence with those specified [24, 34], which constitutes context and has the potential to lead to overall improvement in model performance. To test these hypotheses, we consider incorporating language descriptions as an additional input to monocular depth estimators (see Figure 1). To this end, we investigate the effect of language descriptions of the 3D scene in resolving visual ambiguities and improving the fidelity of monocular depth estimators (MDEs). Amongst them, we consider diffusion-based MDEs, where, despite progress towards high-fidelity reconstructions, they still exhibit error in regions that are textureless or with repetitive textures, as well as objects occupying a small amount of pixels (whether due to their inherent size or their distance from the camera, which scales inversely to their projection onto the image plane). However, training such a model from scratch is prohibitively expensive. To circumvent this computational burden, we leverage diffusion-based MDEs [16, 26, 30], which are a family of MDEs finetuned from pre-trained diffusion models initially intended for text-to-image generation. During text-to-image pre-training, diffusion models learn to generate diverse images under various viewpoints and scene layouts that align with the provided language descriptions. The ability of these models to generate images that align with text suggests that object shapes, sizes, and spatial relationships are implicitly encoded with their latent representation. In fact, such is the primary motivation to re-purpose diffusion models for monocular depth estimation [16, 26, 30]; unlike them, we re-introduce the text descriptions as an input, which has been absent in existing diffusion-based MDEs. When integrating language into the training and inference of diffusion-based MDEs, text is used as a condition. We hypothesize that this will reduce the solution space of possible 3D scenes compatible with a given image, thus improving the accuracy of depth estimates. In our implementation, during the denoising process, both the image and the text input are used by the model to predict the noise to be removed. Ultimately, Gaussian noise is progressively refined into a depth map that aligns with both the input image and the language description. Naturally, this provides the option of inputting different text descriptions to place emphasis on particular objects that may fall into error modes. This study aims to systematically validate the use of language in diffusion-based MDEs and is the first to verify that such MDEs can be improved by integrating with text description as an additional modality. To test our hypothesis, we introduce a text encoder into existing diffusionbased MDEs as they share the same family of text-toimage diffusion models. Amongst them, we examine three different diffusion-based MDEs and their variants: Marigold [30], Lotus [26], and E2E-FT [21]. We conduct training on two synthetic datasets, HyperSim [63] and Virtual KITTI [3], and conduct zero-shot evaluation on five real-world datasets, NYUv2 [69], KITTI [22], ETH3D [68], ScanNet [9], and DIODE [73]. Given the difficulty of obtaining sufficient human-provided text descriptions for every training and inference image, we utilize a visionlanguage model, i.e. LLaVA v1.6 [44] and InternVL3- 8B [108], to generate descriptions for each image, simulating human annotation. To evaluate the influence of text descriptions on diffusion-based MDEs, we begin by (1) evaluating the overall model performance; we expect the incorporation of text as an additional modality to improve the accuracy of the depth maps estimated. As the descriptions include objects that exist within the image, we also test (2) the influence of text inputs on local depth estimates; we expect the biases of objects described to improve regions in which the object lies within the output depth map. ### 2. Related Work

Monocular depth estimation. Monocular depth estimation is a dense regression problem, requiring pixel-wise predictions of depth from a single image, capturing complex spatial relationships within the scene. Some depth models learn to infer pixel-wise depth in metric scale (i.e. meters) [2, 14, 15, 18, 32, 33, 43, 72, 77, 79, 93, 94, 102] by minimizing loss between depth predictions and groundtruth depth maps. Yet, without an additional sensor, e.g., lidar [7, 13, 46, 50–53, 61, 78, 80–83, 90], radar [38, 39, 62, 70, 76], to ground estimates to metric-scale, it is difficult to generalize monocular depth estimation across domains. Each model typically applies to only one data domain in which it is trained, with similar camera parameters and object scales. To enable generalization across diverse domains, empowering depth estimation in the wild, other works have explored affine-invariant depth estimation [11, 30, 42, 58, 59, 87, 88, 91, 92, 96], which predicts depth up to an unknown global shift and scale. This approach can accommodate varied scenes with different scales, while still preserving the geometric relationships between different objects or regions within the scene. HND [96] hierarchically normalizes the depth representations with spatial information and depth distributions. Depth Anything [87] learns from large-scale automatically annotated data. DPT [59] leverages vision transformers using a scale- and shift-invariant trimmed loss. MiDas [58] mixes multiple datasets with training objectives invariant to depth range and scale. Depth Anything [87] and Depth Anything v2 [88] train a depth foundation model using large-scale unlabeled data. Existing methods try to predict depth solely from images, which suffer from ambiguity like scale, object orientation, occlusion, and visual nuisance like viewpoints, illumination, appearance, texture, etc. In this paper, we investigate integrating language into diffusion-based monocular depth estimators to provide conditions of 3D scenes, to resolve the ambiguity and the nuisance variables in the pure visual algorithm. Diffusion model for monocular depth estimation. Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM) [27] learns to reverse a diffusion process that progressively degrades images with Gaussian noise so that they can draw samples from the data distribution by applying the reverse process to random noise. They have been applied to various tasks like text-to-image generation [57, 64, 75, 110, 111], super resolution [20, 37, 65], image inpainting [8, 47, 89], 3D object generation [36, 45, 54], etc. Several approaches have explored the use of DDPM for metric depth estimation. DiffusionDepth [10] learns to denoise a random depth distribution into a depth map with monocular visual conditions. Similarly, DDP [29] encodes the input image and uses diffusion to decode it into a depth map. Depth- Gen [66] extends a multi-task diffusion model for metric depth prediction. Its successor, DDVM [67], focuses on pretraining with synthetic and real datasets to improve depth estimation performance. In light of strong representation learned by pre-trained, text-to-image diffusion models, recent methods like DepthFM [25], VPD [104], E2E- FT [21], GeoWizard [16], Lotus [26], GenPercept [84], Jasmine [74], and Marigold [30] utilize a pretrained Stable Diffusion model [64] as the backbone to predict depth through a denoising process. Specifically, Marigold [30] concatenates input images with diffusion latents as conditions for the denoising U-Net to predict the noise to be removed, gradually denoising Gaussian noise into a depth map. While previous works have primarily focused on using images as the conditioning input to diffusion models for depth prediction, we investigate using language as an additional condition to improve monocular depth estimation. ### 4. Experiments

Training datasets. The training scheme follows the same ones proposed in Marigold [30], Lotus [26], and E2E- FT [21]. All of them are trained on two synthetic datasets that cover both indoor and outdoor scenes. The first dataset utilized is HyperSim [63], a photorealistic compilation of 461 indoor scenes. Marigold and E2E-FT approximately select 54,000 samples, and Lotus filters out incomplete samples to keep approximately 39,000 samples. The second dataset is Virtual KITTI [3, 17], which comprises synthetic street scenes across five distinct settings, with varied conditions such as different weather patterns and camera perspectives. The Virtual KITTI 2 [3] version is used, and for all Marigold, Lotus, and E2E-FT, four scenes are selected, totaling approximately 20,000 samples. Evaluation datasets. Trained models are evaluated using 5 real-world datasets that were not part of their training data: NYUv2 [69], ScanNet [9], KITTI dataset [12, 22, 23], ETH3D [68], and DIODE [73], following Marigold [30], Lotus [26], and E2E-FT [21]. Following the affine-invariant depth evaluation protocol [30, 58, 59, 87, 95], we evaluate first-order threshold accuracy δ1 and mean absolute relative error AbsRel. Details of the evaluation data and metric are provided in the Supplementary Material. Obtain language description. To implement our method, we require human-provided text descriptions for each image. Since standard benchmarks do not include such descriptions, we propose using off-the-shelf models to generate text descriptions to simulate those a human would provide. For this purpose, we use Vision Language Models to generate text. Specifically, we use LLaVA v1.6 [44] for Marigold, and InternVL3-8B [108] for Lotus and E2E-FT. Each training and testing image is generated with one text description. Details of obtaining language descriptions can be found in the Supplementary Material. Implementation details. We follow the setup of original Marigold [30], Lotus [26], and E2E-FT [21]. Details are provided in the supplementary materials. Quantitative comparison. ### 5. Discussion

Conclusion. We investigate the influence of integrating language into diffusion-based depth estimators and find that it enhances monocular depth estimation. Our explanation is that language provides an additional condition (learned during the text-to-image pre-training of diffusion models) associated with plausible 3D scenes, thus reducing the solution space for depth estimation. Limitation. One primary limitation is its dependence on the accuracy and detail of the provided language descriptions. As shown in Figure 9, ambiguous or misleading text inputs from human users can result in suboptimal depth predictions and potentially compromise the model’s performance. Although human-provided descriptions are typically more accurate than automatically generated ones, they can still be incomplete or contain subtle ambiguities. A potential solution is to incorporate a language-robustness module, such as uncertainty estimation or consistency-based filtering, to correct or filter out incorrect descriptions.
